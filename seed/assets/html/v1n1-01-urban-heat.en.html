Urban Heat Islands in Medium-Sized Cities: An Analysis of Landsat Data for 2000–2023

Elena Sokolovskaya1, Artyom Melnikov2, Svetlana Kim1

  • 1 Novobor State University, Russia
  • 2 Polis Graduate School of Urban Studies, Russia

Abstract

Research on urban heat islands has traditionally focused on the largest metropolitan areas, while medium-sized cities, home to a substantial share of the population, remain poorly studied. This study assesses the intensity and dynamics of the surface urban heat island in twelve anonymised cities with populations of 100,000 to 500,000 in the temperate climate zone. Land surface temperature was retrieved from Landsat 5, 7 and 8 scenes for the summer months of 2000–2023, and for each city the difference between the built-up area and a suburban buffer zone was calculated. The normalised difference vegetation index (NDVI), the share of impervious surfaces and building density derived from open map data were used as covariates. The mean daytime heat island intensity was 3.4 °C, and over the study period it increased by 0.9 °C on average. The largest contribution to this change comes from the loss of green space in peripheral areas of new residential development, whereas the association with population size proved weak. A mixed-effects regression model explains 71% of the between-city and between-year variation. The results show that medium-sized cities are no less vulnerable to overheating than large ones and that planning decisions in new development zones are a key adaptation tool. The proposed method can be reproduced for any city using open data.

Keywords: urban heat island; remote sensing; Landsat; land surface temperature; green infrastructure; medium-sized cities

1. Introduction

The urban heat island — the excess of air and land surface temperature in a city over the surrounding rural area — is one of the most extensively studied manifestations of anthropogenic climate transformation. Classic studies attribute this phenomenon to changes in the heat balance: the replacement of vegetation with impervious surfaces reduces evaporation, the geometry of the built environment diminishes nocturnal radiative cooling, and anthropogenic sources add heat to the surface layer.

The overwhelming majority of empirical estimates, however, have been obtained for cities with populations of over one million. Medium-sized cities with 100–500 thousand inhabitants are often regarded as “too small” to develop a pronounced heat island, even though it is precisely in such cities that active construction on the periphery has been observed over the past two decades. The present study fills this gap: we estimate the intensity of the surface heat island in twelve medium-sized cities and identify the factors behind its change over 2000–2023.

2. Materials and Methods

The study covers twelve cities of the temperate zone, designated in the article by the codes C1–C12. For each city, cloud-free scenes for June–August were selected from the Landsat archive (on average 4.6 scenes per city per year, 1,318 scenes in total). Land surface temperature was retrieved from the thermal band with an NDVI-based emissivity correction.

Heat island intensity (HII) was defined as the difference between the median temperature of the built-up area and that of a 5 km wide suburban buffer ring, excluding water bodies. Mean NDVI, the share of impervious surfaces, building density and population size were used as explanatory variables. The relationship was estimated with a mixed-effects regression model with a random intercept for city and a fixed effect for year.

3. Results

The mean daytime HII across the whole sample was 3.4 ± 0.8 °C. The lowest values are characteristic of cities with a well-developed valley network and preserved forest parks (2.1–2.6 °C), and the highest of cities in the steppe zone with dense prefabricated panel housing (4.5–4.9 °C). Fig. 1 shows the dynamics of mean intensity by five-year periods.

In the regression model (Table 1), NDVI has the largest effect: a decrease in the index by 0.1 is associated with an increase in HII of 0.62 °C. The share of impervious surfaces is significant, but its effect is largely mediated by vegetation. Population size is statistically insignificant once morphological indicators are taken into account.

4. Discussion

The intensity estimates obtained are comparable with those published for European cities of similar size and are only 1–1.5 °C lower than the values for the largest agglomerations. This calls into question the widespread notion that heat risk in medium-sized cities is negligible.

The most important finding of the study is the leading role of vegetation on the periphery. New residential neighbourhoods built after 2010 on former agricultural land and wasteland have minimal green cover, and it is here that the increase in surface temperature is most pronounced. A limitation of the study is the use of daytime scenes only: the nocturnal heat island, which is the most significant for health, requires other data sources.

5. Conclusion

Medium-sized cities develop pronounced surface heat islands whose intensity is increasing. Preserving and creating green spaces in areas of new development is the most effective lever for adaptation. The proposed open algorithm can be used by municipalities for monitoring.
